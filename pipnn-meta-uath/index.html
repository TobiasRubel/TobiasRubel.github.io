PiPNN, end to end

PiPNN(X, params):   G ← (X, ∅)   B ← Partition(X)  ▸ overlapping leaves   for leaf b ∈ B in parallel:     Eb ← Pick(b)  ▸ dense in-leaf k-NN candidates     G.PruneAndAddEdges(Eb)  ▸ per-point online prune   if params.final_prune:     for x ∈ X in parallel: RobustPrune(x)   return G

Timing: inside 7:00-18:30 — the act’s first slide, so say the strategy in one breath before the pseudocode: construction is offline, so stop answering one point at a time — partition the data, compare locally, prune online. The slide opens on the bare point set — the input, G ← (X, ∅) — then four advances on one continuous scene: (1) Partition carves the overlapping leaves — point at a lens where two hulls share points; the ringed dots are the leaders; (2) Pick draws each leaf’s 2-NN edges; (3) the online prune sparsifies as the streams merge — do NOT explain it yet; name-drop it (“an online prune we call HashPrune”) and promise it in a few slides: it is the step that makes this pipeline legal — overlapping, large clusters mean long candidate lists, and the cost of taming them lives exactly here; (4) the final RobustPrune pass sparsifies further — it costs ~7-10% of build time and buys ~10% QPS at recall ≥ 0.9, which is why it’s on by default (P69). The retellable version: “partition, compare locally, stream candidates into an online prune.”

Partitioning: overlapping leaves by ball carving

  • Ball carving — sample a random subset of points as partition leaders (the ringed dots); assign every point to its closest leader; any cell larger than C_{\max} (typically 1024) recurses.
  • Overlap via fanout — assign each point to its top-f closest leaders, so close neighbors are not split across a cell boundary.
  • Multi-level fanout — make f a function of depth (e.g. 10 at the top, 3 below).

Multi-level fanout: 1.35–2.5× faster than replication, same quality.

Walk it in the figure’s terms, two frames. Click one, the disjoint tiling: the ringed dots are sampled leaders, cells are closest-leader assignment; recursion keeps cells at cache size (P36). But boundaries split neighbor pairs — the curse of dimensionality at work — so the partition must overlap. Click two swaps the figure to the same scene with fanout on: the cells become genuinely overlapping hulls. Say replication verbally as the blunt baseline: re-run the whole partitioning with fresh random leaders so pairs split by one run’s boundary co-occur in another — it works (the main results ran 1- and 2-replica configs), but every replica pays the full partitioning cost again. Fanout is the cheap overlap: every point joins its top-f leaders in the one pass that already scored them — that’s why the hulls in the still genuinely overlap. Then the refinement: f wide at the top level where cells are coarsest, narrow below — multi-level fanout, same boundary insurance at a fraction of replication’s cost. One more click for the payoff box: 1.35–2.5× faster builds than replication at the same quality (P28; full ablation in backup). Depth if asked: point-to-leader scoring is one blocked dense GEMM; undersized cells merge into siblings; recursion runs 2–3 levels (P43; pseudocode in backup).

Picking from Leaves

  • Inside a leaf, compare everything to everything.
  • Each point keeps its two nearest co-members — directed 2-NN edges.
  • Symmetrize — add back-edges.
  • Two per leaf is enough.

This is where the “dense by design” story lives, now in three clicks synced to the clips. Click one — all-to-all: the full distance matrix of one cache-sized leaf, the exact opposite memory behavior of beam search’s scattered gathers. Click two — each point keeps its two nearest co-members: a directed 2-NN graph, mutual pairs showing as parallel opposite arrows. Click three — symmetrize: the missing back-edges arrive and the graph STAYS directed — bi-directed means both directions present; the paper’s read is that forward and backward edges each contribute to traversability. Click four, said over the frozen graph: two per leaf suffices because overlap accumulates — a point in many leaves collects candidates from all of them (P67). Depth if asked lives in backup (the v1 Eigen + Highway kernel); the follow-up work in Part V replaces that kernel outright with a quantized fused one. Handoff: every leaf now streams its small symmetrized 2-NN graph out as candidate edges — thousands of leaves, in parallel. Pruning that firehose is the next slide’s problem.

The pruning problem

  • The union of all leaf graphs is dense
  • Much of it is redundant — near-parallel edges, plus back-edges piling into hubs.
  • We must prune — but the classical prune (RobustPrune) is offline: complete candidate set, globally sorted; buffering the parallel streams is slow and memory-hungry.

Needed: an online prune in bounded memory.

The act’s turn, now motivated visually in three clicks. Click one — the union: eight leaves’ tidy little graphs, unioned; overlap means every point collects candidates from many leaves, and the tangle is the result (in the real build it’s thousands of leaves, not eight). Click two — the redundancy: the orange set is every edge dominated by a nearer, same-direction neighbor — near-parallel duplicates all over the graph, and where back-edges pile up you get hubs (the flashes); this is HCNNG’s trap — union everything, never prune online — and it pays in dense redundant adjacency lists and query quality (backup slide). Click three — what pruning must produce: exactly the orange set drops, few diverse edges remain. So we must prune — but RobustPrune, DiskANN’s rule, the one every search-built method uses (α rule precisely in backup), is offline: complete candidate set, globally sorted, before any decision; our candidates arrive as parallel streams from leaves racing each other, and buffering them first gives up the streaming win and holds huge lists in memory. Click four, read the marked line as a spec: online, in bounded memory. The next slide is the data structure that meets it — and insertion-order independence arrives as its theorem.

HashPrune: an online pruning reservoir

Per source point p: hash each candidate’s residual direction c - p. On a bucket collision, keep the nearer; when full, evict the farthest.

  • 8-byte slots (4-byte id · 2-byte hash · 2-byte bf16 norm); reservoir maintenance is at most ~5% of construction time.

Walk the loop: residual directions from p; each candidate hashes to a bucket — one of the LSH cones, pointed at the source. Beat one: a nearer arrival collides and evicts the farther occupant of its bucket. Beat two: a farther arrival bounces off the full reservoir. Say the parameters verbally: m = 12 hyperplanes in practice (P44). The buckets do the diversity job that global sorting did for RobustPrune. Tick the spec off as you talk: online — candidates insert as they arrive; bounded — ℓ slots per point; order-independence is the theorem, next slide. This is the box inside PruneAndAddEdges from the walkthrough — the streams you already watched feed exactly this loop. Depth if asked: reservoir maintenance stays at or under about five percent of total construction time (P32).

HashPrune is history-independent


Theorem. For a fixed hash function h_p, candidate set C, and reservoir size \ell, the final adjacency list produced by HashPrune is unique — independent of the insertion order of candidates in C.

Corollary. Fixing the random seeds for partitioning, PiPNN is deterministic — the same index on every build, regardless of thread scheduling.

Let it land, then move on: candidates can arrive from thousands of leaves, in any order, and the graph comes out the same — the last requirement of the spec, and the reason the walkthrough’s racing parallel streams are safe: no schedule to control, no lock order to reason about, no nondeterminism in the output. Then the corollary, one click later: the partitioner has the same property (full version proves both components deterministic and history-independent, P77), so the whole build is reproducible — fix the seeds and a 192-core racy build produces bit-identical indexes every run. That’s rare for parallel ANN builders, and auditors and debuggers love it. If asked, the proof sketch and the shuffled-order animation are in backup: buckets keep their D(p,·)-minimum, eviction keeps the ℓ nearest bucket winners, and neither rule mentions arrival order. Close the act here; the next act is evidence.

Top-k is the majority of the work

  • Leaf building: each point finds its top-2 inside the leaf.

Same kernel twice: score a dense block, keep the top k.

Read the breakdown: partitioning is the largest share on three of the four datasets — 57, 56 and 67 percent — and leaf building takes the rest, except on OpenAI where the 1536-dimensional leaves flip it to 54. CPU: quantize, then fuse the top-k

  • Quantize so the scores run on integer SIMD dot products — byte codes needed for VNNI; less memory traffic too.
  • We use TurboQuant variant (fast Walsh-Hadamard rotation + Lloyd-Max Codebook) - could use others
  • Fuse scoring with top-k selection — the distance matrix is never materialized.

About 30 seconds; a transition, not an explanation. Top row, PiPNN I: score the fp32 vectors with a GEMM, store the whole distance matrix, then sort every row for its top-2. Bottom row, this paper. Bullet one: quantization’s job here is mostly to feed the hardware — byte codes are what the integer dot-product instructions want, VNNI on CPU, WGMMA on GPU — and it cuts memory traffic, which is what binds on the GPU at high dimension (P85). Bullet two: the kernels are written against a template, integer inner product plus a small per-vector correction, so the choice of quantizer is secondary — RaBitQ, DRIVE and EDEN would plug into the same kernels (P86); our TurboQuant variant replaces the residual QJL sketch with an extra codebook bit plus a per-vector rescale (P51). Bit width does still matter: TQ4 misses the recall floor on five GPU datasets, which is why the GPU headline uses RSQ8 (P88). Bullet three, fusion: each score drops straight into the top-2 and the matrix is never stored. Instruction-level detail is in backup. Next: what those two moves do to the kernel, stage by stage.

Backup: history-independence — proof sketch

Theorem For a fixed hash function h_p, candidate set C, and reservoir size \ell, the final adjacency list produced by HashPrune is unique — independent of the insertion order of candidates in C.

Proof sketch (i) The collision rule means bucket b ends up holding the D(p,\cdot)-minimum among candidates hashing to b. (ii) The eviction rule always removes the farthest, so the reservoir ends as the \ell nearest bucket winners. Both sets are determined by (h_p, C, \ell) alone — order appears nowhere. \square  Full proof: paper’s full version (arXiv).

Backup: RobustPrune

RobustPrune(p, C, α) Sort C by D(p,\cdot). Repeat until |N(p)| = R or C is empty: move the nearest remaining y into N(p), then delete every remaining z with \alpha \cdot D(y,z) \;<\; D(p,z), \qquad \alpha \ge 1. Introduced in DiskANN (Subramanya et al., NeurIPS ’19).

Backup — cut from the main line in feedback round 2 (this room already knows RobustPrune); keep for Q&A if someone asks for the α rule. The triangle, with the α dial: the test edge y→z is stretched by α before comparing against the direct edge. α = 1 is exactly the classical RNG condition (the RNG-condition backup slide, Arya & Mount ’93); larger α prunes less, keeping longer detour-cutting edges. The load-bearing fact — RobustPrune needs the complete candidate set, globally sorted, before any decision — is stated on the main line’s pruning-problem slide.

Backup: HashPrune, as implemented

Insert(p, candidate c, dist d):
  if full and d ≥ farthest: return  ▸ early reject
  h ← sign sketch of (c − p)  ▸ m = 12 bits, precomputed
  pos ← binary-search slots by h
  if collision at h: keep the nearer of the two
  elif not full: shift-insert at pos
  else: overwrite the farthest, restore hash order

  • One reservoir per point; each slot is 8 bytes: 4-byte id · 2-byte hash · 2-byte bf16 norm.
  • Slots stay sorted by hash — insert is a binary search, not a scan.
  • Reservoir maintenance is ≤ ~5% of total construction time.

Backup: the RNG condition

Domination A candidate c is dominated given kept neighbors N(p) if \exists\, b \in N(p): \quad D(p,b) \;<\; D(p,c) \;\;\text{and}\;\; D(b,c) \;<\; D(p,c) — a kept neighbor nearer to p is also closer to c than p is: points nearer to p prune those farther away. The RNG rule keeps only the undominated edges (Arya & Mount ’93).

One triangle is the whole idea: the green edge to b plus the short hop b→c makes the long red edge redundant. Diversity falls out: near-collinear candidates dominate each other. This is RobustPrune’s α = 1 case.

Backup: HNSW in detail

  1. Layered graph; each point draws a maximum level from a geometric distribution.
  2. Insert by greedy descent from the top layer to the point’s level.
  3. On each layer below: a candidate search of the partial layer graph (efConstruction), then RNG-based selection — the domination rule on the previous backup slide — keeps M diverse neighbors; degrees are capped.

Queries descend the layers the same way.

Backup: NSG in detail

  1. Start from an approximate k-NN graph (itself built by another method).
  2. For each point: search from a navigating node over that graph to collect candidates.
  3. Select edges with an RNG-style rule (MRNG); patch connectivity with a spanning tree from the navigator.

No incremental insertion here — NSG refines a prebuilt graph in a single pass — yet candidate generation is still search.

Backup: Vamana (DiskANN)

Backup: score with one instruction

vpdpbusd (AVX-512 VNNI) does 64 multiply-accumulates per instruction — 16 int32 lanes × 4 byte pairs — decoded quantized codes in, int32 scores out, in registers.

One vpdpbusd: sixteen int32 lanes, four byte pairs each, sixty-four MACs — the sixty-four cells in the panel on screen. Layout (P53): database codes are pre-transposed into panels of sixteen points so each i32 lane owns a distinct point, and the query’s matching 4-byte group is broadcast into every lane. The catch someone always asks: one operand is unsigned, so database codes are stored shifted by +128 and the bias — 128 times the query byte-sum — is subtracted afterwards. Unrolling: six queries by four panels, twenty-four vpdpbusd per iteration, one broadcast amortized over four panels, six independent accumulator chains. Top-k side: displacement sort — per-lane trackers in registers, horizontal merge deferred to the end; k=3 stays entirely in registers, k=10 spills to L1, affordable because top-10 runs only at the root. The leaf instance and its ablation are the next backups.

Backup: AssignLeaders — top-f against a small leader set

  • Partitioning’s kernel is asymmetric: every point scores against a small leader set — so pretranspose the leaders once and stream cache-sized chunks of points through.
  • The fanout schedule sets k: top-10 at the root, top-3 below, top-1 deeper.
  • Top-1 is an in-register argmin sweep. Top-3 / top-10 use displacement sort: each SIMD lane tracks its own top-k in k registers, and the horizontal merge is deferred to the very end.
  • k = 3 stays entirely in registers; k = 10 spills to L1 — affordable, because only the root scores each point once while lower levels score every replica.

The loop body is where the instruction-level parallelism lives: every four dimensions the CPU kernel issues thirty-two of those instructions — eight queries across four sixteen-point panels — each into its own accumulator, so thirty-two independent dependency chains hide the instruction latency; that is what fixed the transposed-layout regression in the ablation. Every sixty-four dimensions the GPU issues two tensor-core instructions into one accumulator and waits — no chain trick needed, the instruction is already huge, and the overlap comes from TMA having the next stripes in the ring. Feeding: the CPU relies on L1 and the prefetcher, with queries pre-broadcast so the inner loop does plain loads; the GPU has TMA filling a four-stage ring with producer and consumer warps. Select: both fuse selection into the score loop — the CPU per block with two top-2 trackers over the lower triangle, the GPU per finished tile in registers with a four-lane shuffle merge over the full square, because tensor cores want dense rectangles. Click, the mark: same shape on both — score a tile, select in registers, never store the scores. That is the thesis of the talk, in silicon.

Backup: why PiPNN doesn’t just port to the GPU

  1. Recursive partitioning — a dynamic tree of dependent launches instead of flat bulk passes.
  2. Skewed bucket sizes — a launch finishes when its slowest block does: oversized leaves stall the grid while near-empty ones idle SMs.
  3. Lock-based HashPrune — thousands of concurrent inserts contending on per-point reservoirs serialize on locks.

Fixes: flatten · regularize · segment · sort-and-merge.

Backup: flatten — recursion becomes two sorted rounds

Sample leaders, assign each point to its f nearest, radix-sort by leader key — every bucket becomes a contiguous run. Two rounds suffice (f_0 = 8, then f_1 = 4).

The Q&A slide for how the recursion disappears. One point: the recursion tree becomes flat bulk work. Two rounds suffice, fanout 8 then 4, giving up to 32 leaf memberships, about 30 in practice. The price is one device-wide sort per round: bulk, regular work the GPU is happy to do.

Backup: regularize bucket sizes

  • After sorting, run-length-encode the bucket sizes, then split the oversized and merge the undersized toward a target of ≈ 1024 points.
  • What it buys: every leaf now fits one fixed tile shape — a single kernel serves all of them, with no per-size specialization and no relaunches.
  • The slowest-block problem dissolves: uniform buckets mean no straggler stalls the grid.

Backup: one fused, segmented top-k kernel

  • Every leaf is a segment of the same launch — one kernel scores them all.
  • Inside: producer warps TMA-stage the next 32-byte stripe into shared memory while WGMMA consumes the current one — the copy hides under the math, one matmul group always in flight.
  • Scores drain from accumulators straight into register top-k, merged at the end (butterfly): the score matrix never exists in global memory.

The CPU fusion, at warpgroup scale.

Backup: sort-and-merge HashPrune, step by step

  • Thousands of concurrent inserts on per-point reservoirs would serialize on locks — so emit now, resolve later: write two direction-records per candidate edge into a buffer.
  • Radix-sort the batch by owner and distance, then realize every reservoir in one sequential merge sweep.
  • The license is the theorem: HashPrune is order-free, so a sorted batch and a stream of locked inserts land on the same final reservoir.

Ammo for the two questions this always draws. Memory: the per-batch record slab is capped at about 6 GB in the paper’s runs, so large builds are processed in batches — the cap is a memory-budget knob, not a correctness one. Cost: the sort plus merge is the epilogue of each round and is bulk-parallel; it is not the bottleneck of the build, which stays in the segmented scoring kernel.

Backup: any order, same reservoir

Legal because HashPrune is history-independent: the sorted batch lands on the same final reservoir as a stream of locked inserts.

The payoff of the theorem, for Q&A: two rows, the same twelve records in two arrival orders, both collapse to the identical reservoir. That is why the GPU build is bit-identical to the locked CPU build.

Backup: DirectEdgePrune — prune only what was witnessed

  • One search-shaped pass remains: the final RobustPrune wants candidate-to-candidate distances the build never computed — recomputing them is a scatter-gather pass.
  • The fix: prune using already-witnessed distances only. The error is one-sided — it can only miss prunes, never over-prune.
  • The trade: graphs come out 30–58% denser at comparable query quality, and GPU build time drops by up to 1.4×.

Backup: speed of light on the H100

  • GPiPNN’s leaf-2-NN kernel is L2-cache-bandwidth-limited at d ≥ 1536 (OpenAI-2M, DBpedia-3072): 82–95% of unified memory throughput, while DRAM stays at 37–52% and the SM pipeline holds at 46–49% — below NCU’s 60% compute-bound threshold.

The honest answer to “is the kernel good?”: at high dimension we are within 5-18% of the memory system’s roof, so there is little left on the table there; the remaining headroom is at low dimension, where the pipeline is not saturated either.

Backup: distributed related work

  • Existing distributed vector-search systems (LANNS, Pyramid, SPANN, and others) concentrate on serving, not construction
  • They build per shard (no cross-shard edges — quality suffers) or on one machine, then distribute (no scale benefit at build time)
  • Distributed construction of one graph with cross-partition edges has received little attention
  • Insertion-by-search makes a distributed beam search costly; PiPNN’s partition-local structure avoids it

Ongoing work — an update batch is a small dataset built into an existing partition

  • Persist the partition (leaders at every level) and the HashPrune reservoirs
  • Insert: route Δ through the persisted tree; per affected leaf score |Δb|·|b|, not |b|²
  • Stream edges into both endpoints’ reservoirs — no graph traversal
  • Delete: drop from reservoirs and re-prune from what remains; repair only overflowing/underflowing subtrees

History-independence does the work here: because HashPrune’s reservoir is a function of the candidate set, not its arrival order, the leaves touched by a batch end up with exactly the reservoir a from-scratch build over X union Delta would have produced — no coordination with the rest of the graph required. Concretely: a new batch is routed through the persisted leader tree exactly as at build time; for each affected leaf only the new-versus-old block is scored, not the full leaf again; edges stream into the reservoirs of both endpoints. Deletion drops a point from the reservoirs that hold it and re-prunes from what is left — most of the time no new distance computation, because reservoirs are kept above the final degree. Drift is handled per subtree: resample and rebuild only where a subtree has overflowed, which costs a small PiPNN build, now seconds to minutes. Full rebuild is always the fallback, and it is fast enough to be one.

Ongoing work — distributed PiPNN: four rounds, no coordination

  1. Sample + broadcast leaders
  2. Assign + shuffle: top-F₀ per point, quantized codes only
  3. Local build: partition · pick · HashPrune per machine
  4. Shuffle + merge reservoirs at home

Constant rounds; no coordination between machines.

The merge is associative and commutative — the theorem doing distributed work, the same one that made HashPrune insertion-order-independent on one machine. Four bulk-synchronous rounds, constant in the MPC model: sample top-level leaders on each machine and broadcast the union; assign each local point to its top-F0 leaders and shuffle its quantized code to the owning machine; run the rest of PiPNN — partition, pick, HashPrune — locally on each machine’s subproblem; then shuffle partial reservoirs back to each point’s home machine, and merge by the HashPrune rule. Balance by bin-packing bucket sizes onto machines; keep F0 lean, the way the SISAP lean-partition-plus-refinement result suggests, and recover quality with a refinement pass instead of a rich top level.
