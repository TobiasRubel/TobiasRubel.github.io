Part II PiPNN: search-free construction

PiPNN: Ultra-Scalable Graph-Based Nearest Neighbor Indexing — KDD 2026

One click before speaking the act: Pip fades in — the corgi PiPNN is named after. Let the room have the beat. Then the strategy in one breath: construction is offline, so stop answering one point at a time — partition the data, compare locally, prune online.

PiPNN, end to end

Partitioning: overlapping leaves by ball carving

  • Ball carving — sample a random subset of points as partition leaders (the ringed dots); assign every point to its closest leader; any cell larger than C_{\max} (typically 1024) recurses.
  • Overlap via fanout — assign each point to its top-f closest leaders, so close neighbors are not split across a cell boundary.
  • Multi-level fanout — make f a function of depth (e.g. 10 at the top, 3 below).

Leader assignment is a top-k problem: score every point against the leaders, keep its top-f.

About 45 seconds, walked in the figure’s terms. Click one, the disjoint tiling: ringed dots are sampled leaders, cells are closest-leader assignment, recursion keeps cells at cache size. Boundaries split neighbor pairs, so the partition must overlap. Click two, the same scene with fanout on: overlapping hulls. Replication — rerun the whole partitioning with fresh leaders — is the blunt version; fanout is the cheap one: every point joins its top-f leaders in the pass that already scored them, 1.35 to 2.5 times faster than replication at the same quality (P28; ablation in backup). Click three: f wide at the top, narrow below. Click four, the mark, read verbatim: leader assignment is a top-k problem — score every point against the leaders, keep its top-f. One dense block, a top-f on every row. Hold the shape: the leaves have it too, next slide, and the second half of the talk is about making it fast.

Picking from leaves

  • Each point keeps its two nearest co-members — directed 2-NN edges.
  • Symmetrize — add back-edges.
  • Two per leaf is enough.

About 45 seconds; the setup for the whole second half. Click one, all-to-all: the full distance matrix of one cache-sized leaf — the exact opposite memory behavior of beam search’s scattered gathers. Click two, each point keeps its two nearest co-members. Click three, symmetrize — the back-edges arrive, the graph stays directed. Click four, over the frozen graph: two per leaf suffices because a point sits in many leaves and collects candidates from all of them. Click five, the marked line, read verbatim: a top-k problem — score every pair, keep each point’s k nearest. That shape is what the second half of the talk accelerates. Handoff: every leaf streams its small graph out as candidate edges, thousands of leaves in parallel; pruning that firehose — briefly, it is the KDD paper’s — is next.

HashPrune: an online, order-independent prune

  • The union of the leaf graphs is dense and redundant — and the classical prune (RobustPrune) is offline: complete candidate set, globally sorted.
  • HashPrune — per source point p: hash each candidate’s residual direction c - p; on a bucket collision, keep the nearer; when full, evict the farthest. Online, in bounded memory.
  • Theorem. The final adjacency list is independent of insertion order. With fixed seeds, PiPNN is deterministic.

About 60 seconds — the act’s turn and its hinge on one slide; this room knows RobustPrune, so spend the time on the theorem. Click one, the problem: every point sits in many leaves, so the union of the leaf graphs is a dense tangle, much of it redundant — near-parallel edges, back-edges piling into hubs. We must prune, and RobustPrune, the rule every search-built method uses, is offline — complete candidate set, globally sorted, before any decision — while our candidates arrive as parallel streams from thousands of leaves. Click two, HashPrune, walked over the loop on the right: residual directions from p; each candidate hashes to one of the LSH cones, and two candidates in the same cone are near-parallel — exactly the redundancy. A nearer arrival evicts the farther occupant; a farther arrival bounces off a full reservoir. Online, bounded memory. Parameters if asked: m = 12 hyperplanes, only 6 bits ever hurt, 8 bytes per slot, maintenance at or under about five percent of build time (P44, P32). Click three, the theorem, and do not rush it: for a fixed hash function and candidate set the final adjacency list does not depend on insertion order — so with fixed seeds a 192-core racy build is bit-identical every run (P8, P77). Click four, the hook: if the reservoir does not depend on arrival order, any procedure that produces the same candidate set is legal — including sorting the whole batch on a GPU and merging it in one sweep. That is the GPU act. Proof sketch, the full pruning-problem clips and the shuffled-order clip are in backup.

Read the breakdown: partitioning is the largest share on three of the four datasets — 57, 56 and 67 percent — and leaf building takes the rest, except on OpenAI where the 1536-dimensional leaves flip it to 54. CPU: quantize, then fuse the top-k

  • Quantize so the scores run on integer SIMD dot products — byte codes needed for VNNI; less memory traffic too.
  • We use TurboQuant variant (fast Walsh-Hadamard rotation + Lloyd-Max Codebook) - could use others
  • Fuse scoring with top-k selection — the distance matrix is never materialized.

About 30 seconds; a transition, not an explanation. Top row, PiPNN I: score the fp32 vectors with a GEMM, store the whole distance matrix, then sort every row for its top-2. Bottom row, this paper. Bullet one: quantization’s job here is mostly to feed the hardware — byte codes are what the integer dot-product instructions want, VNNI on CPU, WGMMA on GPU — and it cuts memory traffic, which is what binds on the GPU at high dimension (P85). Bullet two, for the quantization people in the room: the kernels are written against a template, integer inner product plus a small per-vector correction, so the choice of quantizer is secondary — RaBitQ, DRIVE and EDEN would plug into the same kernels (P86); our TurboQuant variant replaces the residual QJL sketch with an extra codebook bit plus a per-vector rescale (P51). Bit width does still matter: TQ4 misses the recall floor on five GPU datasets, which is why the GPU headline uses RSQ8 (P88). Bullet three, fusion: each score drops straight into the top-2 and the matrix is never stored. Instruction-level detail is in backup. Next: what those two moves do to the kernel, stage by stage.

GPiPNN: keep the tensor cores fed

  • Regularize the leaves — cap and pack them — so every leaf is one contiguous segment of a dense slab and no block straggles.
  • Feed asynchronously — TMA streams tiles into a shared-memory ring while the tensor cores consume the current stripe.
  • Score with WGMMA, select in registers — the score tile is never written to memory.

Everything else becomes a sort — HashPrune included: sort candidates by owner and distance, merge once, no locks. Legal because HashPrune is order-independent.

Timing: 10:00-12:00 for the GPU act — three slides; this one is about 35 seconds, over the clip. The goal on a GPU is one thing: keep the tensor cores fed. PiPNN as written cannot — recursion, skewed leaves, locked reservoirs (P35). Three moves. Regularize, the clip: sort the points by leaf, cap the big leaves, pack the small siblings, so a batch of leaves is one dense slab, every leaf a contiguous segment, and no block straggles behind the launch (P59). Feed asynchronously: TMA copies the next tile into a shared-memory ring while the tensor cores consume the current 64-dimension stripe — producer warps load, consumer warps compute (P60). Score with WGMMA and select in registers: a 64-by-128 score tile is reduced over the whole dimension, then each lane keeps its top-2 in registers and four lanes merge by shuffle; the score tile is never written to memory (P60). The closer, quietly: everything else became a sort — recursion is a fixed number of assign-then-sort rounds (P58), and HashPrune is sort by owner and distance, then one merge pass, no locks — legal because the reservoir is order-independent, the theorem from the middle of the talk (P61, P8). Leaf scoring still dominates GPU time, less than on CPU (P91). If asked about leaf sizes: capped and packed, not equalized. If asked about the merge key: sort by owner, then distance, then one pass — leave the bit layout alone. The tile-shape table — queries by points by dims per instruction on each platform — is in backup for the kernel questions. Then the numbers.

Backup: GPU baselines — the tuning protocol

  • For each (method, dataset) pair we swept parameters and kept one configuration: for the ANNS index, the fastest build whose QPS-recall curve matches an unquantized ParlayANN-Vamana graph by area under the curve; for k-NN graphs, the fastest build reaching graph recall ≥ 0.9 (5,000 seeded points against brute-force top-10; “–” if nothing clears the floor).

  • RaBitQ was not used during queries for any method, including Jasper — no method gets a query-side quantization advantage.
  • Asymmetry we should state plainly: on the k-NN task CAGRA is fixed at 32/128 on every dataset, while GPiPNN’s k-NN parameters are swept per dataset.

Backup: H100 in two instructions

  • WGMMA — a warpgroup (128 threads) collectively issues one tensor-core matrix multiply: a 64 × 128 tile, advanced 32 contraction-bytes per step, int8 × int8 → int32 — the GPU sibling of vpdpbusd.
  • TMA — the tensor memory accelerator copies whole tiles between global and shared memory from a descriptor (base, shape, stride, swizzle), instead of per-thread address arithmetic; it stages the database slab without consuming warp registers.

Goal: overlap computation and memory movement — keep the tensor cores busy.

Vocabulary only, and only if asked: a warp is 32 threads, a warpgroup is 4 warps, M = 64 is fixed by the ISA and N = 128 is our tiling choice. The quantization template from the CPU act is what makes int8 × int8 the right currency here.

Backup: what one step feeds — CPU vs GPU

One instruction 1 query × 16 points × 4 dims 64 queries × 128 points × 32 dims
Per dimension step (loop body) every 4 dims: 8 queries × 4 panels → 32 instructions into 32 independent accumulators every 64 dims: 2 instructions into one accumulator, then wait
Feeding the dimensions L1 + hardware prefetch; queries pre-broadcast TMA → 4-stage shared-memory ring; producer / consumer warps
Select fused per block; two top-2 trackers; lower triangle per finished tile, in registers; 4-lane shuffle merge; full square

Backup, for the kernel questions; read the table across, one row per breath. One instruction: on CPU, one query against sixteen points over four dimensions — sixteen four-dimensional dot products in one instruction; on GPU, a warpgroup instruction scores sixty-four queries against one hundred twenty-eight points over thirty-two dimensions. The loop body is where the instruction-level parallelism lives: every four dimensions the CPU kernel issues thirty-two of those instructions — eight queries across four sixteen-point panels — each into its own accumulator, so thirty-two independent dependency chains hide the instruction latency; that is what fixed the transposed-layout regression in the ablation. Every sixty-four dimensions the GPU issues two tensor-core instructions into one accumulator and waits — no chain trick needed, the instruction is already huge, and the overlap comes from TMA having the next stripes in the ring. Feeding: the CPU relies on L1 and the prefetcher, with queries pre-broadcast so the inner loop does plain loads; the GPU has TMA filling a four-stage ring with producer and consumer warps. Select: both fuse selection into the score loop — the CPU per block with two top-2 trackers over the lower triangle, the GPU per finished tile in registers with a four-lane shuffle merge over the full square, because tensor cores want dense rectangles. Click, the mark: same shape on both — score a tile, select in registers, never store the scores. That is the thesis of the talk, in silicon.

Backup: speed of light on the H100

  • GPiPNN’s leaf-2-NN kernel is L2-cache-bandwidth-limited at d ≥ 1536 (OpenAI-2M, DBpedia-3072): 82–95% of unified memory throughput, while DRAM stays at 37–52% and the SM pipeline holds at 46–49% — below NCU’s 60% compute-bound threshold.

The honest answer to “is the kernel good?”: at high dimension we are within 5-18% of the memory system’s roof, so there is little left on the table there; the remaining headroom is at low dimension, where the pipeline is not saturated either.

Backup: the quantization codes

  • Rotate first, then quantize per coordinate — no coordinate is special afterwards.
  • RSQ8 — 8-bit rotated scalar quantization.
  • TQ4 — TurboQuant’s 4-bit Lloyd–Max codebook on rotated coordinates, with its inner-product estimator.
  • Our TQ4 variant: replaces the residual (QJL) sketch with an extra codebook bit plus a per-vector rescale; 4-bit codes decode to int8 in-register (vpshufb).

The slide for “what exactly is your TQ4?” — TurboQuant’s authors are in the room, so be precise about the delta and generous about the lineage: both codes rotate first; RSQ8 is one byte per rotated coordinate; TQ4 is TurboQuant’s 4-bit Lloyd-Max codebook with the inner-product estimator; our variant swaps the residual QJL sketch for an extra codebook bit plus a per-vector rescale, and the 16-entry codebook decodes to int8 in-register with vpshufb.

Backup: the template is the contribution

Distance = integer inner product + a small per-vector correction. Anything fitting it rides the same kernels — VNNI on CPU, WGMMA on GPU.

  • RaBitQ, DRIVE and EDEN all fit the same template — distance = integer inner product + a small per-vector correction — and could be substituted for our quantizer without touching the kernels.
  • We picked the TurboQuant variant for fast, data-oblivious O(d \log d) encoding and because its decoded codes map cleanly onto integer dot-product instructions: VNNI on CPU, WGMMA on GPU.
  • In the GPU comparisons, RaBitQ is disabled at query time for every method, Jasper included.

The answer to “why not RaBitQ?” — because the paper’s claim is about the kernel template, not the quantizer, and RaBitQ satisfies the template. Do not offer a head-to-head recall comparison from the appendix: that subsection is inside a hidden block and is not part of the compiled paper.

Backup: the CPU kernel — quantize, then fuse

  • Quantize the vectors — TQ4 (TurboQuant) or RSQ8: a distance becomes an integer dot product plus a small per-vector correction.
  • Fuse scoring with selection: each score goes straight into the top-k; the distance matrix is never materialized.
  • Quality holds: QPS ratios 0.98 (TQ4) / 0.99 (RSQ8) vs. FP32 at matched recall.

The Q&A slide for “what exactly did you do in the kernel?”: quantize (TQ4 or RSQ8 — a distance becomes an integer dot product plus a per-vector correction), fuse (each score goes straight into the top-k tracker; the distance matrix is never materialized), and the receipt (QPS ratios 0.98 and 0.99 versus FP32 at matched recall, P14). The instruction — vpdpbusd, AVX-512 VNNI — and the panel layout are on the next backup slide.

Backup: score with one instruction

vpdpbusd (AVX-512 VNNI) does 64 multiply-accumulates per instruction — 16 int32 lanes × 4 byte pairs — decoded quantized codes in, int32 scores out, in registers.

One vpdpbusd: sixteen int32 lanes, four byte pairs each, sixty-four MACs — the sixty-four cells in the panel on screen. Layout (P53): database codes are pre-transposed into panels of sixteen points so each i32 lane owns a distinct point, and the query’s matching 4-byte group is broadcast into every lane. The catch someone always asks: one operand is unsigned, so database codes are stored shifted by +128 and the bias — 128 times the query byte-sum — is subtracted afterwards. Unrolling: six queries by four panels, twenty-four vpdpbusd per iteration, one broadcast amortized over four panels, six independent accumulator chains. Top-k side: displacement sort — per-lane trackers in registers, horizontal merge deferred to the end; k=3 stays entirely in registers, k=10 spills to L1, affordable because top-10 runs only at the root. The leaf instance and its ablation are the next backups.

Backup: RobustPrune

RobustPrune(p, C, α) Sort C by D(p,\cdot). Repeat until |N(p)| = R or C is empty: move the nearest remaining y into N(p), then delete every remaining z with \alpha \cdot D(y,z) \;<\; D(p,z), \qquad \alpha \ge 1. Introduced in DiskANN (Subramanya et al., NeurIPS ’19); \alpha = 1 is the classical RNG condition.

Backup: the pruning problem

  • The union of all leaf graphs is dense — and much of it is redundant: near-parallel edges, plus back-edges piling into hubs.
  • We must prune — but the classical prune (RobustPrune) is offline: complete candidate set, globally sorted; buffering the parallel streams is slow and memory-hungry.

Needed: an online prune in bounded memory.

Backup (Q&A: “why not just union the leaf graphs?”). Click one, the union: eight leaves’ tidy graphs unioned — overlap means every point collects candidates from many leaves, and the tangle is the result; in a real build it is thousands of leaves. Over the frozen frame: a large fraction of those edges are dominated — a nearer neighbor in the same direction already covers them — and back-edges pile into hubs. That is HCNNG’s trap: union everything, never prune online, pay in dense adjacency lists and query quality. Click two, what pruning must produce: the dominated set drops, the diverse edges remain. But RobustPrune, the rule every search-built method uses, is offline: complete candidate set, globally sorted, before any decision — and our candidates arrive as parallel streams from leaves racing each other. Click three, read the mark as a spec: online, bounded memory. Next slide is the data structure that meets it.

Backup: HashPrune — the insertion loop

Per source point p: hash each candidate’s residual direction c - p. On a bucket collision, keep the nearer; when full, evict the farthest.


Online, in bounded memory: the buckets do the diversity job of RobustPrune’s global sort.

Backup. Walk the loop: residual directions from p; each candidate hashes to a bucket — one of the LSH cones, pointed at the source; two candidates in the same cone are near-parallel, exactly the redundancy the previous slide showed. Beat one: a nearer arrival collides and evicts the farther occupant. Beat two: a farther arrival bounces off a full reservoir. Parameters verbally: m = 12 hyperplanes, and only 6 bits ever hurt (P44); 8 bytes per slot, maintenance at or under about five percent of construction time (P32). Tick the spec: online, candidates insert as they arrive; bounded, fixed slots per point. The third property — the one this room should care about — is the theorem on the next slide.

Backup: HashPrune is history-independent


Theorem. For a fixed hash function h_p, candidate set C, and reservoir size \ell, the final adjacency list produced by HashPrune is unique — independent of the insertion order of candidates in C.

Corollary. Fixing the random seeds for partitioning, PiPNN is deterministic — the same index on every build, regardless of thread scheduling.

Backup, the full statement. Candidates can arrive from thousands of leaves in any order and the graph comes out the same: no schedule to control, no lock order to reason about. Click one, the corollary: the partitioner has the same property, so with fixed seeds a 192-core racy build produces bit-identical indexes every run. Proof sketch next: buckets keep their distance-minimum, eviction keeps the nearest bucket winners, neither rule mentions arrival order.

Backup: history-independence — proof sketch

Theorem For a fixed hash function h_p, candidate set C, and reservoir size \ell, the final adjacency list produced by HashPrune is unique — independent of the insertion order of candidates in C.

Proof sketch (i) The collision rule means bucket b ends up holding the D(p,\cdot)-minimum among candidates hashing to b. (ii) The eviction rule always removes the farthest, so the reservoir ends as the \ell nearest bucket winners. Both sets are determined by (h_p, C, \ell) alone — order appears nowhere. \square  Full proof: the paper’s full version (arXiv).

Backup: AssignLeaders — top-f against a small leader set

  • Partitioning’s kernel is asymmetric: every point scores against a small leader set — so pretranspose the leaders once and stream cache-sized chunks of points through.
  • The fanout schedule sets k: top-10 at the root, top-3 below, top-1 deeper.
  • Top-1 is an in-register argmin sweep. Top-3 / top-10 use displacement sort: each SIMD lane tracks its own top-k in k registers, and the horizontal merge is deferred to the very end.
  • k = 3 stays entirely in registers; k = 10 spills to L1 — affordable, because only the root scores each point once while lower levels score every replica.

Backup: the leaf kernel — a fused self-join

  • Inside a leaf every point is both query and database — so score only the lower triangle: half the distance work.
  • Each score updates two top-2 trackers at once: the query row’s (in registers) and the database point’s (loaded once per panel, updated in-register, stored back).
  • Lane-parallel top-2 insertion runs in 6 blends; masking retires self-pairs and padding.
  • The output is leaf-2NN: each member’s two nearest co-members, symmetrized into the candidate stream HashPrune absorbs.

Backup: why PiPNN doesn’t just port to the GPU

  1. Recursive partitioning — a dynamic tree of dependent launches instead of flat bulk passes.
  2. Skewed bucket sizes — a launch finishes when its slowest block does: oversized leaves stall the grid while near-empty ones idle SMs.
  3. Lock-based HashPrune — thousands of concurrent inserts contending on per-point reservoirs serialize on locks.

Fixes: flatten · regularize · segment · sort-and-merge.

Backup: flatten — recursion becomes two sorted rounds

Sample leaders, assign each point to its f nearest, radix-sort by leader key — every bucket becomes a contiguous run. Two rounds suffice (f_0 = 8, then f_1 = 4).

The Q&A slide for how the recursion disappears. One point: the recursion tree becomes flat bulk work. Sample leaders, assign every point to its f nearest, radix-sort by the induced leader key so each bucket is a contiguous run — read the picture left to right, twice. Two rounds suffice, fanout 8 then 4, giving up to 32 leaf memberships, about 30 in practice. The price is one device-wide sort per round: bulk, regular work the GPU is happy to do.

Backup: regularize bucket sizes

  • After sorting, run-length-encode the bucket sizes, then split the oversized and merge the undersized toward a target of ≈ 1024 points.
  • What it buys: every leaf now fits one fixed tile shape — a single kernel serves all of them, with no per-size specialization and no relaunches.
  • The slowest-block problem dissolves: uniform buckets mean no straggler stalls the grid.

Backup: one fused, segmented top-k kernel

  • Every leaf is a segment of the same launch — one kernel scores them all.
  • Inside: producer warps TMA-stage the next 32-byte stripe into shared memory while WGMMA consumes the current one — the copy hides under the math, one matmul group always in flight.
  • Scores drain from accumulators straight into register top-k, merged at the end (butterfly): the score matrix never exists in global memory.

The CPU fusion, at warpgroup scale.

Backup: sort-and-merge HashPrune, step by step

  • Thousands of concurrent inserts on per-point reservoirs would serialize on locks — so emit now, resolve later: write two direction-records per candidate edge into a buffer.
  • Radix-sort the batch by owner and distance, then realize every reservoir in one sequential merge sweep.
  • The license is the theorem: HashPrune is order-free, so a sorted batch and a stream of locked inserts land on the same final reservoir.

Ammo for the two questions this always draws. Memory: the per-batch record slab is capped at about 6 GB in the paper’s runs, so large builds are processed in batches — the cap is a memory-budget knob, not a correctness one. Cost: the sort plus merge is the epilogue of each round and is bulk-parallel; it is not the bottleneck of the build, which stays in the segmented scoring kernel.

Backup: any order, same reservoir

Legal because HashPrune is history-independent: the sorted batch lands on the same final reservoir as a stream of locked inserts.

The payoff of the theorem, for Q&A: two rows, the same twelve records in two arrival orders, both collapse to the identical reservoir. That is why the GPU build is bit-identical to the locked CPU build.

Backup: DirectEdgePrune — prune only what was witnessed

  • One search-shaped pass remains: the final RobustPrune wants candidate-to-candidate distances the build never computed — recomputing them is a scatter-gather pass.
  • The fix: prune using already-witnessed distances only. The error is one-sided — it can only miss prunes, never over-prune.
  • The trade: graphs come out 30–58% denser at comparable query quality, and GPU build time drops by up to 1.4×.
